How the numbers are worked out
  1. Surprise. Actual minus forecast. The actual is the figure first announced on the day, not today's revised number, because that is what markets reacted to. The forecast is the consensus shown in TradingView's economic calendar just before the release.
  2. Day 1 move. From the market's last close before the release to its close on release day. US data comes out at 13:30 London (12:30 when the US and UK clocks are out of step), so day 1 covers the reaction and the rest of that session. S&P 500 futures, the dollar index and gold are in %; the 10-year yield is in basis points (1bp = 0.01 percentage points).
  3. The next 10 days. The same starting point, measured to each of the next 10 closes. The dashed line is the median (middle) path of every past release with the same kind of surprise; the shaded band holds the middle half of them.
  4. Held, kept going or faded. We compare the move after 5 trading days with the day 1 move. Kept going: same direction and bigger. Held: same direction and at least half of it still there. Faded: less than half left, or it reversed. Day 1 moves smaller than are left out as too small to call. Same rule as the Earnings Moves page.
  5. Busy days. Marked ! in the table: a Fed decision later the same day, or another release on this page out at the same time. Use Leave out busy days to drop them from every number on the page.
  6. Rose on the day. The share of releases where the market closed higher on release day than the close before. For the 10-year yield, "rose" means the yield went up (bond prices went down).

What this can't tell you

  • A day's close includes everything else that happened that day. Some release days also had a Fed decision, other data or big news. The intraday replay shows the first reaction on its own.
  • Past patterns are a guide to how markets have behaved, not a forecast.
  • Some releases are small samples (GDP has about 48 since 2015).
